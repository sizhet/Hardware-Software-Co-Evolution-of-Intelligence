## Abstract

Modern artificial intelligence is commonly built upon an implicit **representation-first assumption**:

> the world is sensed, converted into a general internal representation, and then processed by an intelligent computational system.

This architecture has been enormously successful, but it may describe only part of the intelligence landscape.

Biological evolution suggests another possibility.

Long before organisms possessed rich internal representations, general-purpose neural computation, symbolic reasoning, or language-like cognition, physical structures could already selectively respond to environmental differences. Receptor tuning, mechanical coupling, resonance, threshold effects, specialized sensory structures, and dedicated neural pathways could amplify some signals while suppressing others.

Such mechanisms need not fully represent **what something is** before affecting **what the organism does next**.

This paper proposes a broader framework:

> **Intelligence does not necessarily begin with representation. It may begin when a physical or sensory difference acquires structural consequence.**

A difference may become structurally consequential when it changes downstream activation, routing, state, computation, attention, control, or behavior.

From this perspective, hardware intelligence is not merely infrastructure underneath AI. Physical and sensory structures may themselves participate in intelligent selection, salience formation, triggering, routing, and structural organization.

The paper therefore proposes **Hardware–Software Co-Evolution of Intelligence (HSCI)**: a multi-substrate view in which intelligent functions may be distributed across physical structures, sensors, analog mechanisms, Boolean logic, structural triggering, artificial neural networks, world models, large language models, and future Brain-Unit AI systems.

The central engineering question consequently changes from:

> **Which model should perform this intelligence?**

to:

> **Where should this intelligent distinction be physically and computationally realized?**

This leads to the principle of **Per-Function Intelligence Placement**: intelligent functions should be placed at the earliest and smallest sufficient substrate consistent with energy, latency, reliability, flexibility, and computational requirements.

The resulting framework connects evolutionary intelligence, hardware specialization, structural intelligence, Function Tunnels, emergency intelligence, autonomous systems, and Brain-Unit AI within a common architectural landscape.

# 1. Introduction

Artificial intelligence is usually discussed as computation.

A typical conceptual pipeline is:

```text
World
  ↓
Sensor
  ↓
Digital Representation
  ↓
AI Model
  ↓
Reasoning / Decision
  ↓
Action
```

Within this architecture, the sensor belongs to hardware engineering, while intelligence begins somewhere after the signal has been transformed into a machine-readable representation.

This separation is convenient.

It is not necessarily fundamental.

Consider a system in which a physical structure selectively responds to one environmental pattern, strongly amplifies it, suppresses irrelevant alternatives, and directly changes downstream routing or behavior.

Has intelligence really not begun?

The answer depends on what is meant by intelligence.

If intelligence requires explicit semantic representation, symbolic reasoning, or general-purpose computation, then the answer is no.

If intelligence includes:

* selective response,
* relevance detection,
* salience formation,
* triggering,
* routing,
* state transition,
* structural organization,
* and behaviorally meaningful discrimination,

then some primitive form of intelligence may already exist.

This motivates the central question of HSCI:

> **Why should intelligence begin only after representation?**

# 3. The Evolutionary Puzzle

Biological evolution did not begin with a general-purpose representation engine.

Early organisms faced immediate survival problems:

```text
light / dark
hot / cold
chemical / non-chemical
food / non-food
approach / avoid
stable / dangerous
```

Natural selection did not need to solve:

> How can we build a universal representation of the environment?

It needed to solve much smaller problems:

> Can this structural change improve survival or reproduction?

A useful evolutionary modification could therefore be extremely local:

```text
Environmental Difference
        ↓
Specialized Physical Response
        ↓
Signal Amplification
        ↓
Behavioral Change
        ↓
Fitness Advantage
```

No general world representation is required.

No universal symbolic system is required.

No large computational architecture is required.

Evolution can accumulate small structural differences because each successful difference may already have local utility.

# 4. Evolutionary MET

From an engineering perspective, evolutionary selection can be interpreted as a form of **MET-like optimization**.

A simplified conceptual objective might be expressed as:

$$
\text{Evolutionary Value}
\sim
\frac{
\Delta \text{Survival} + \Delta \text{Reproduction}
}{
\Delta \text{Energy}
+
\Delta \text{Complexity}
+
\Delta \text{Structural Cost}
}
$$

This is not intended as a literal biological fitness equation.

It is a structural abstraction.

The important point is that evolution does not optimize for:

```text
Maximum General Intelligence
```

in isolation.

It operates under constraints such as:

```text
Energy
Material
Size
Development
Mutation
Latency
Reliability
Environment
Reproduction
```

Under these constraints, specialized physical intelligence may sometimes be substantially cheaper than general computation.

Instead of:

```text
General Sensor
      ↓
Universal Representation
      ↓
General Computation
      ↓
Decision
```

evolution may favor:

```text
Specialized Structure
      ↓
Selective Coupling
      ↓
Direct Salience
      ↓
Trigger
      ↓
Behavior
```

This suggests a basic principle:

> **Do not compute what physical structure can already select.**

# 7. Physical Difference and Structural Consequence

Not every physical response should be called intelligence.

A photon producing an electrical signal is not automatically an intelligent event.

A filter attenuating a frequency is not automatically an intelligent agent.

The important transition occurs when a physical difference begins to produce a meaningful structural effect downstream.

We therefore distinguish:

```text
Signal Transformation
        ↓
Physical Difference
        ↓
Selective Response
        ↓
------------------------------
Structural Consequence
        ↓
Routing / State / Trigger
        ↓
Behavior
```

This transition will be developed formally in HSCI-002 as the:

# Structural Consequence Boundary — SCB

A preliminary definition is:

> **The Structural Consequence Boundary is the transition at which a physical or sensory difference begins to alter downstream computational structure, routing, state, control, or action.**

The SCB is not necessarily a single physical line.

It may be a transition region.

This matters because intelligence itself may emerge as a gradient rather than appearing instantaneously at one architectural boundary.

# 15. Per-Function Intelligence Placement

This leads to one of the main engineering principles of HSCI:

# Per-Function Intelligence Placement

Given an intelligent function \(F\), the system should not automatically assume that \(F\) belongs in an ANN, LLM, or general-purpose processor.

Instead, choose an implementation substrate \(P\):

$$
P^*(F)
=
\arg\min_{P}
J(F,P)
$$

where a conceptual cost function may include:

$$
J(F,P)
=
\alpha E
+
\beta L
+
\gamma C
+
\delta R
+
\epsilon W
+
\zeta A
$$

with:

* \(E\): energy cost,
* \(L\): latency,
* \(C\): computational cost,
* \(R\): reliability or risk cost,
* \(W\): weight or physical cost,
* \(A\): adaptation / flexibility cost.

Candidate placements may include:

```text
Physical Structure
Sensor
Analog Circuit
Event Detector
Boolean Logic
CCC
ANN
World Model
LLM
```

The weights depend on the application.

A data-center reasoning system and an autonomous micro-drone should not optimize the same placement objective.

---

# 16. The Smallest Sufficient Intelligence Principle

Per-Function Intelligence Placement suggests a practical design principle:

> **Put the smallest sufficient intelligence at the earliest effective layer.**

For example, if collision risk can be reliably detected by an inexpensive event-driven sensor and a simple structural trigger, there may be no reason to activate a large vision model before beginning an emergency response.

Likewise:

> **Do not ask an ANN to rediscover a distinction that a sensor can physically expose.**

And:

> **Do not ask an LLM to perform a decision that a deterministic structural trigger can safely perform.**

This is not an argument against ANN or LLM intelligence.

It is an argument against unnecessary computational escalation.

Can multi-substrate intelligence outperform LLM-centered architectures in efficiency, reliability, and structural control?

# 27. What HSCI Does Not Claim

This framework intentionally avoids several excessive claims.

HSCI does **not** claim that:

1. every sensor is intelligent;
2. every physical response is cognition;
3. resonance automatically implies pleasure;
4. hardware intelligence should replace software intelligence;
5. biological evolution can be reduced to a simple optimization equation;
6. ANN or LLM computation is unnecessary;
7. pre-representational intelligence is sufficient for general intelligence;
8. all useful intelligence should be pushed into hardware;
9. a world model is unnecessary for autonomous intelligence;
10. present autonomous systems already implement the full HSCI architecture.

Instead, HSCI proposes a narrower and testable hypothesis:

> **Some intelligent selection can occur before general representation, and some of that selection can be physically embodied.**

The engineering consequence is equally specific:

> **AI architecture should treat the placement of intelligence across substrates as an explicit design variable.**

# 29. Core Principles

The HSCI framework can be summarized by the following principles.

## Principle 1 — Intelligence Before Representation

> Intelligence does not necessarily begin with a complete representation of the world.

## Principle 2 — Structural Consequence

> A physical difference becomes intelligence-relevant when it begins to alter downstream structure, routing, state, control, or behavior.

## Principle 3 — Physical Participation

> Physical structure can participate directly in intelligent selection.

## Principle 4 — Multi-Substrate Intelligence

> Intelligence can be distributed across physical, sensory, structural, neural, symbolic, and generative substrates.

## Principle 5 — Smallest Sufficient Intelligence

> Put the smallest sufficient intelligence at the earliest effective layer.

## Principle 6 — Intelligence Placement

> Do not assume that every intelligent distinction belongs in an ANN or LLM.

## Principle 7 — Multi-Timescale Intelligence

> Intelligence need not share one substrate, one representation, or one clock.

## Principle 8 — Structural Consolidation

> Repeatedly successful flexible intelligence can become a candidate for cheaper and more specialized structural realization.

## Principle 9 — Selective Escalation

> Expensive intelligence should be activated when simpler substrates cannot safely resolve the problem.

## Principle 10 — Co-Evolution

> Hardware and software intelligence are not competing paradigms; they can form a continuous co-evolutionary loop.

# 31. Conclusion

Artificial intelligence has largely grown within a representation-first computational tradition.

That tradition remains powerful.

But it may not define the full boundary of intelligence.

Biological evolution suggests that useful intelligent distinctions can begin earlier:

```text
Physical Difference
        ↓
Selective Coupling
        ↓
Resonance
        ↓
Salience
        ↓
Structural Consequence
        ↓
Trigger
        ↓
Representation
        ↓
Cognition
```

This leads to the foundational proposition of HSCI:

> **Intelligence may begin not when a system knows what something is, but when a difference acquires structural consequence.**

Once this possibility is admitted, hardware intelligence gains a natural place inside AI architecture.

Sensors, physical structures, analog mechanisms, Boolean logic, CCC triggering, neural networks, world models, LLMs, and Brain-Unit systems can be understood as different substrates on which intelligent distinctions may be realized.

The central problem is therefore not:

> Hardware or software?

It is:

> **Which intelligence should be placed where?**

Hardware–Software Co-Evolution of Intelligence proposes that future AI systems may improve not only by increasing model size and computation, but also by distributing intelligence more effectively across physical structures, computational substrates, and timescales.

The resulting design principle is simple:

> **Do not compute what physics can already select.**

And its broader architectural counterpart is:

> **The future of AI may depend not only on building larger models, but on placing smaller forms of intelligence at the right physical and structural locations.**
